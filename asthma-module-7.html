Acute Exacerbations & Emergency Management

Most asthma deaths are preventable. Updated for GINA 2026, this module covers structured severity assessment, targeted oxygen, bronchodilator safety, early corticosteroids, IV magnesium, anaphylaxis recognition, disposition, and discharge planning — because what happens after the acute episode determines what happens next.

Learning Objectives — Module 7
By the end of this module, the learner will be able to:
  • Assess acute asthma severity using structured clinical features and, for children and adolescents, a validated score such as PRAM when feasible
  • Apply GINA 2026 oxygen guidance: avoid reflex oxygen; give supplemental oxygen for hypoxemia and titrate to appropriate targets
  • Describe first-hour therapy: inhaled SABA, ipratropium for moderate/severe presentations, and early systemic corticosteroids
  • Recognize SABA toxicity, including lactic acidosis, as a possible contributor to persistent tachypnea after repeated albuterol
  • Identify when to use IV magnesium sulfate and when to call for ICU/PICU support
  • Recognize anaphylaxis with wheeze and prioritize epinephrine before bronchodilators
  • List discharge requirements after an exacerbation, including ICS-containing therapy, technique review, written action plan, and early follow-up

Why This Module Matters

Asthma exacerbations — sudden worsening of symptoms, lung function, and airway obstruction — are the most dangerous acute events in asthma management. They are responsible for the majority of asthma hospitalizations, ED visits, and deaths. The vast majority of fatal asthma events involve patients who had been under-treated, who had inadequate access to care, or who were discharged without appropriate follow-up and controller optimization.

Being able to rapidly assess severity, initiate appropriate therapy in the right sequence, and safely manage the post-acute period is a core competency for every pediatrician, internist, emergency physician, and family medicine trainee. This module gives you the framework to do all three.

Resident Pearl — Severity Is a Pattern, Not a Vibe
Do not rely on “looks okay” alone. In acute asthma, combine speech, work of breathing, oxygen saturation, air entry, mental status, and response to therapy. In children and adolescents, anchor your assessment with a validated score such as PRAM when feasible.

GINA 2026 Updates for Acute Asthma

GINA 2026 places new emphasis on structured assessment and safer escalation during acute asthma care. For SPEC learners, the practical shift is simple: assess systematically, treat early, avoid reflexive over-treatment, and build a safer discharge plan before the patient leaves.

What changed or needs emphasis in 2026?
Use a structured pediatric score: For children and adolescents presenting with an exacerbation, use a validated score such as PRAM when feasible.

Oxygen is targeted: Do not give oxygen reflexively to every wheezing child. Supplemental oxygen is recommended when room-air SpO₂ is below 92% or if the presentation is severe/life-threatening; titrate rather than overshoot.

Anaphylaxis changes the order: If wheeze occurs with features of anaphylaxis, give epinephrine first, then bronchodilators.

Repeated SABA has toxicity: Persistent tachypnea after heavy albuterol may reflect ongoing obstruction, fatigue, complications, or SABA-associated lactic acidosis.

Discharge is prevention: Before discharge, address controller therapy, inhaler technique, action plan, medication supply, and follow-up.

Oxygen and Severity — GINA 2026 Practical Point
Room-air SpO₂ <92% suggests a severe presentation and should prompt oxygen plus close reassessment. Values <90% are especially concerning and should trigger aggressive therapy and early escalation. If oxygen is needed in adults, adolescents, or children 6–11 years, titrate to a controlled target and avoid unnecessary hyperoxia; for children 5 years and younger, target at least 92% while reassessing the full clinical picture.

Oxygen — Targeted, Not Reflexive

GINA 2026 emphasizes controlled oxygen. Supplemental oxygen is not required for every patient with wheeze; it is used for hypoxemia or severe/life-threatening presentations and then titrated to a safe target.

  • If room-air SpO₂ is below 92%, start supplemental oxygen and reassess frequently
  • For adults, adolescents, and children 6–11 years who require oxygen, avoid overshooting; upper target is generally 95%
  • For children 5 years and younger, aim for SpO₂ ≥92% if oxygen is required
  • Increasing oxygen requirement despite bronchodilator therapy is an escalation signal, not a reason to simply keep waiting
Resident Pearl — Treat Hypoxemia, Not Anxiety
A comfortable child with normal oxygen saturation does not need reflex oxygen. A child with worsening work of breathing, poor air entry, or falling saturation needs rapid treatment and reassessment.

Bronchodilator Therapy

Short-acting beta-2 agonist (SABA) bronchodilators are the cornerstone of acute asthma management. The goal is rapid airway smooth muscle relaxation to reduce obstruction acutely while other therapies address the underlying inflammation.

Ipratropium Bromide in Severe Exacerbations

Adding ipratropium bromide to SABA is most useful early in moderate-to-severe presentations. It should generally be used during the initial acute phase, then stopped once the early treatment window has passed unless a local pathway directs otherwise.

SABA Safety: Do Not Miss Lactic Acidosis

Repeated or continuous albuterol can be lifesaving, but overtreatment can also cause toxicity. If tachypnea persists after heavy SABA exposure, reassess the whole patient: persistent obstruction, fatigue, pneumothorax, anaphylaxis, pneumonia, metabolic acidosis, and SABA-associated lactic acidosis are all possibilities.

Clinical Reassessment Question
Is the patient breathing fast because the asthma is still obstructive, because they are tiring, or because repeated beta-agonist therapy has contributed to lactic acidosis? Do not automatically give more SABA without reassessing air movement, mental status, oxygen need, perfusion, and trajectory.

Wheeze + Anaphylaxis — Epinephrine First

Not every child with wheeze has isolated asthma. If wheeze occurs with hives, angioedema, vomiting, hypotension, exposure to a likely allergen, or other features of anaphylaxis, treat anaphylaxis first.

  • Give epinephrine first when anaphylaxis features are present
  • Then give inhaled bronchodilators for bronchospasm
  • Do not delay epinephrine while trying repeated albuterol
Resident Pearl — Wheeze Is Not Always Asthma
Wheeze plus anaphylaxis is anaphylaxis until proven otherwise. Epinephrine first, bronchodilator second.

Systemic Corticosteroids

Systemic corticosteroids are anti-inflammatory treatments that reduce the airway mucosal edema and secretion component of an acute exacerbation. Their onset of action is delayed (4–6 hours for clinical effect), which is why they must be given early — ideally within the first hour of presentation — not held until bronchodilator response is assessed.

GINA 2026 — Systemic Corticosteroid Dosing (Pediatric)
Prednisolone (oral, preferred if tolerated): 1–2 mg/kg/day, maximum 40 mg per day. Duration: 3–5 days for most children — no need for tapering after short courses.

Methylprednisolone (IV, if oral not tolerated or vomiting): 1 mg/kg IV every 6 hours. Equivalent systemic effect to oral prednisolone when administered correctly.

Dexamethasone (alternative): 0.6 mg/kg/day for 1–2 days (max 16 mg); evidence supports equivalence to longer prednisolone courses in some studies; longer half-life means shorter course may be sufficient.

Oral prednisolone is preferred over IV corticosteroids when the patient can swallow and is not vomiting — bioavailability is equivalent and oral administration avoids venous access requirements. A shorter dexamethasone course is an appropriate alternative to a 5-day prednisolone course in children, particularly to improve adherence with the oral regimen.

Resident Pearl — Give OCS Early
Systemic corticosteroids in acute asthma reduce hospitalizations when given within 1 hour of presentation. The most common error is giving three bronchodilator treatments and waiting to "see if the SABA works" before starting steroids. In moderate or severe exacerbations, give both simultaneously — bronchodilator for immediate relief, corticosteroid for the underlying inflammatory surge that caused the episode.

IV Magnesium Sulfate

Intravenous magnesium sulfate is a bronchodilator that works by blocking calcium-mediated smooth muscle contraction. It is recommended by GINA 2026 for severe exacerbations that do not respond adequately to initial intensive bronchodilator and corticosteroid therapy.

IV Magnesium Dosing in Pediatric Severe Asthma
Dose: 25–75 mg/kg IV (maximum 2.5 g) over 20 minutes in children.
Indication: Severe acute asthma with inadequate response to initial intensive bronchodilator therapy; SpO₂ <92% after 1 hour of treatment; FEV₁ or PEF <50% after initial treatment.
Contraindications: Hypermagnesemia (rare); renal failure (relative). Monitor for hypotension and respiratory depression during infusion.
Evidence: Meta-analyses demonstrate reduced hospitalizations in children with severe acute asthma treated with IV magnesium.

Nebulized magnesium sulfate has been studied as an alternative to IV magnesium, with some evidence of benefit in severe pediatric asthma. Its use is generally reserved for centers with established protocols. It is not yet a standard GINA 2026 recommendation as primary therapy but may be considered where IV access is difficult to establish.

Disposition — Discharge vs. Admit vs. ICU

Disposition decisions after initial acute management should be based on the clinical response at 1 hour, not on the initial severity alone. Many patients who present with moderate severity will respond and be safe for discharge; some who present with mild severity may fail to respond and require admission.

Specific Indications for ICU Transfer

  • Silent chest (absent breath sounds) — airflow so reduced that wheeze is gone; pre-arrest sign
  • Cyanosis or persistent hypoxemia despite high FiO₂
  • Decreasing level of consciousness, confusion, or agitation not responding to treatment
  • Respiratory fatigue — labored breathing becoming less forceful, suggesting impending respiratory failure
  • Bradycardia (in severe asthma, this is a late and ominous sign)
  • Requiring continuous nebulized bronchodilator with no improving trend

Discharge Requirements

Safe discharge from the ED or hospital after an acute asthma exacerbation is not simply about clinical stability. GINA 2026 is explicit about what must be in place before the patient leaves. This post-exacerbation window is a critical teachable moment — and the interventions at discharge are among the highest-yield actions for preventing the next exacerbation.

  • Prescribe or step up ICS-containing controller therapy — the patient must leave with a controller prescription and understand they must take it. For adolescents/adults, consider switching to Track 1 (ICS-formoterol) at this visit.
  • Prescribe prednisolone course to complete at home — if not already completed inpatient; 3–5 day oral course for children at 1–2 mg/kg/day (max 40 mg)
  • Check and correct inhaler technique before discharge — do not assume the technique is correct; watch the patient use the device
  • Provide a written asthma action plan — green/yellow/red zone instructions; when to start oral steroids; when to go to ED. Verbal instructions alone are insufficient.
  • Arrange follow-up within 1–3 days for children, with longer-term reassessment in 2–3 months when appropriate — not weeks or months. The immediate post-exacerbation period is high risk for recurrence.
Teach Families This
Why We're Sending You Home With More Medicine, Not Less
"I know it may seem strange that we are adding or strengthening daily asthma medicine after an emergency visit — but this is how we prevent the next one. The rescue inhaler opens the airways quickly, but the controller medicine treats the inflammation that made this flare happen. We want to check technique today and see you again soon to make sure recovery is on track."

Case Vignette

Module 7 — Acute Management Case
9-Year-Old in the Emergency Department — What Do You Do First?
Zoe, age 9, arrives to the ED with her father. She is speaking in phrases, sitting upright, mildly agitated. RR 28, HR 116, SpO₂ 91% on room air. Bilateral expiratory wheeze, mild accessory muscle use at the sternocleidomastoids. PEF 55% of personal best. Her father says she used her albuterol inhaler "about 6 times at home over the past 3 hours" without much improvement.

Classify severity. Write your first-hour management orders. When will you reassess? What triggers ICU notification?

Severity classification: Moderate (phrases, agitated, SpO₂ 91% — below the 92% severe-concern threshold — PEF 55%). Classify by worst feature: SpO₂ 91% is moderate-severe borderline for a child; PEF 55% is moderate. Overall: moderate, trending toward severe given lack of response to 6 home SABA doses.

First-hour management orders:

1. Supplemental oxygen: nasal cannula because SpO₂ is below 92%; titrate and reassess rather than overshoot.

2. Albuterol 0.15 mg/kg (minimum 2.5 mg) via nebulizer every 20 minutes × 3 doses — add ipratropium bromide 250 mcg per nebulizer treatment × 3 doses (first 3 treatments).

3. Prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg orally (max 40 mg) — give NOW, do not wait for bronchodilator response assessment.

4. Continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring and pulse oximetry.

5. Establish IV access (anticipating potential need for IV corticosteroids or magnesium).

Reassessment: After all 3 bronchodilator treatments (approximately 60 minutes) — reassess SpO₂, RR, HR, work of breathing, speech pattern, and PEF.

ICU notification triggers: If Zoe develops decreasing level of consciousness, silent chest, cyanosis, bradycardia, inability to speak, rising CO₂ concern, or persistent hypoxemia despite oxygen — notify intensivist immediately. Do not wait for a further clinical deterioration before calling ICU; call early when the trajectory is concerning.

Self-Assessment

Question 1 of 3
An 8-year-old with acute asthma has SpO₂ 91% on room air, speaks in phrases, and has moderate retractions. What is the best oxygen decision?
  • A. Give oxygen to 100% saturation because maximum oxygenation is always safest
  • B. Start supplemental oxygen because SpO₂ is below 92%, then titrate and reassess
  • C. Avoid oxygen unless SpO₂ falls below 85%
  • D. Oxygen is unnecessary if wheeze is audible
Correct Answer: B GINA 2026 emphasizes controlled oxygen. SpO₂ below 92% suggests a severe presentation and supports supplemental oxygen with frequent reassessment. The goal is targeted oxygen, not reflex hyperoxia.
Question 2 of 3
A 7-year-old has moderate acute asthma in the ED. After 3 rounds of albuterol with no significant improvement, the nurse asks about additional medications. Which add-on therapy has the strongest evidence for reducing hospitalizations in this scenario?
  • A. Add-on inhaled ICS (extra doses of budesonide via nebulizer)
  • B. IV magnesium sulfate (25–75 mg/kg, max 2.5g over 20 min) — appropriate after failure of initial intensive bronchodilator therapy
  • C. Oral aminophylline (theophylline) — a bronchodilator with a narrow therapeutic window; use routinely as step-up therapy
  • D. Additional ipratropium doses — continue ipratropium beyond the first 3 treatments for maximum bronchodilation
Correct Answer: B IV magnesium sulfate has meta-analytic evidence supporting its use in severe pediatric acute asthma, demonstrating reduced hospitalizations compared with placebo. GINA 2026 includes IV magnesium as an escalation option when a severe presentation fails to respond adequately to initial bronchodilator and corticosteroid therapy. Dose: 25–75 mg/kg (max 2.5 g) over 20 minutes. Option D is incorrect — the benefit of ipratropium is greatest in the first 3 doses; continuing beyond this has not demonstrated added benefit and may increase side effects. Aminophylline (Option C) has a narrow therapeutic window, significant adverse effects, and is not recommended as routine step-up therapy per GINA 2026.
Question 3 of 3
A 10-year-old with moderate asthma has responded well in the ED — SpO₂ 97%, PEF 72%, comfortable at rest. She is being discharged. Which element is MOST critical to complete before she leaves?
  • A. Repeat chest X-ray to confirm resolution
  • B. Provide verbal instructions about when to return to ED
  • C. Ensure ICS-containing controller is prescribed or stepped up, inhaler technique is checked, a written asthma action plan is provided, and early follow-up is arranged
  • D. Prescribe a 10-day antibiotic course for the probable viral trigger
Correct Answer: C GINA 2026 is explicit about discharge requirements: (1) prescribe or step up ICS-containing controller; (2) oral prednisolone course if not completed; (3) check inhaler technique; (4) provide written asthma action plan; (5) arrange early follow-up, often within 1–3 days for children. All five elements matter — but the bundled approach of controller + technique check + action plan + follow-up is the most impactful discharge intervention. Chest X-ray is not routinely indicated in uncomplicated acute asthma. Verbal instructions alone are insufficient. Antibiotics are not indicated for viral-triggered exacerbations without confirmed bacterial superinfection.
